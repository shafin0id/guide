## Abstract

Enterprises still hesitate to let large language model agents run multi-step work on their own, because a plausible final answer does not show that task constraints survived every hand-off or that tool actions followed policy. This paper studies that gap with a design science research artefact: a runtime that adds evidence-based agent routing, signed hash-linked hand-offs, an external allow, bound or deny policy gate, and a linked execution trace to a baseline multi-agent pipeline. The artefact was compared against a fixed-role sequential baseline and a static-graph baseline across three independent model families (DeepSeek, Google Gemini and an open-weight model served by Groq), using eighteen enterprise-style tasks run under nominal conditions and four tasks run under an assumed compromise of one agent. On nominal tasks, task success sat at a ceiling of 93.5 to 100 percent for the two capable models, so the controls produced no measurable gain there, at governance overhead of about 1.5 milliseconds per run against several seconds of model latency. Under assumed compromise, the ungated baselines leaked restricted data or executed an unauthorised action in 100 percent of relevant trials across all three models, the policy gate blocked out-of-domain actions in every trial, and a provenance guard was the only mechanism that stopped an attacker steering output to an in-domain but unapproved recipient. This paper also derives a static bound on the effects a compromised subset of agents can cause before any run executes, proves the bound sound against the enforcing runtime, confirms it exactly against a live adaptive attacker on direct effects, and shows a synthesised least-privilege policy cuts the bound's sink capabilities by more than half while denying no legitimate task. The findings hold across all three model families under the tested conditions, and the static bound extends two concurrent 2026 preprints rather than being the first of its kind. The main limitation is that the task suite and its checkers were authored by the research team rather than drawn from an external benchmark.

Keywords: agentic AI security, prompt injection, least privilege, information flow control, design science research, tool authorisation

## 1. Introduction

Most enterprises still do not let large language model agents complete multi-step work without a human checking every step. The reason is not model quality. A multi-agent pipeline can produce a fluent, complete-looking answer while quietly dropping a constraint at a hand-off, routing a step to a weak agent, or asking a tool to do something nobody authorised. A reviewer reading the final answer has no way to tell. This is a workflow-control problem, and it sits next to the model-quality problem rather than inside it.

The risk is concrete. In a procurement review split across three agents, one agent reads vendor data, one checks the evidence, and one writes the recommendation. The instruction "do not disclose supplier pricing" has to survive two hand-offs before the recommendation is written. If an intermediate agent restates the data in its own words, the restriction can disappear without any agent doing anything that looks wrong on its own. The same gap applies to tool use: an agent asked to look something up in a change-management system can also, in the same turn, ask the system to delete a record, and nothing about a fluent natural-language reply reveals whether that second request was authorised. Prior work on multi-agent debate reports exactly this kind of drift, where task constraints degrade as the conversation grows (Becker et al., 2026), and indirect prompt injection research shows that content an agent reads, not just content a user types, can redirect what an agent does (Liu et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024).

Three lines of existing work each address part of the problem but not the whole of it. Adaptive routing and graph-structured orchestration improve which agent gets a step and reduce some context loss (Ji et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), but routing research is evaluated on task accuracy, not on whether a constraint or a permission survived the hand-off. Tool-authorisation and information-flow research enforces rules on individual tool calls at run time, with strong recent examples such as CaMeL (Debenedetti et al., 2025) and FIDES (Costa et al., 2025), but almost all of this work assumes one trusted planning agent rather than a pipeline where any agent in the chain might itself be compromised. Static, pre-deployment analysis of what a compromised agent could do is newer still: Parisel (2026) screens a multi-agent configuration for influence and collusion but states no enforcement guarantee, and Muruaga (2026) proves that a runtime authorisation scope cannot grow along a delegation chain, which is a property of the running system rather than a bound computed before deployment. None of these three lines evaluates its claims against more than one model family, so whether a finding is a property of the architecture or an artefact of one model remains, in most of this literature, untested.

This paper reports four contributions.

1. An open design science research artefact that integrates evidence-based agent routing, signed hash-linked hand-offs, an external allow, bound or deny policy gate, and a linked execution trace in one orchestration runtime. The combination of these four controls was independently published in September 2026 (Paul and Nandy, 2026; Safin et al., 2026), so this paper presents the artefact as a design science contribution rather than as a first integration.
2. A pre-registered, condition-blind comparison of the artefact against a fixed-role sequential baseline and a static-graph baseline, repeated across three model families, under both nominal task conditions and an assumed compromise of one agent in the pipeline.
3. A static, compromise-parametric bound on the effects a compromised subset of agents can cause, proved sound against the runtime that enforces it, checked exactly against a live adaptive attacker on direct effects, and paired with a least-privilege policy synthesis procedure. This extends Parisel (2026), Muruaga (2026) and Moon and Varshney (2026) rather than preceding them, as the systematic review in Section 2.5 explains.
4. Honestly reported negative and null results: a ceiling effect on nominal task success that leaves no room for the controls to show a gain, a finding that textual prompt injection could not be measured as a threat because the tested models ignored every injected instruction, and a heuristic provenance mechanism that is shown, by the same static analysis, to be bypassable under one attack variant.

The remainder of the paper reviews related work in Section 2, describes the artefact and the evaluation protocol in Section 3, reports results in Section 4, discusses what the results mean and where they fall short in Section 5, and concludes in Section 6.

The aim of this study is to evaluate whether a policy-aware, traceable orchestration layer improves the reliability and auditability of enterprise large language model workflows enough to justify its overhead, and whether its security benefit, if any, is specific to one model or holds across model families. The research questions are listed below, following directly from the approved proposal, with one addition.

- RQ1: What requirements are needed for a policy-aware, traceable framework for enterprise large language model multi-agent workflows?
- RQ2: How can adaptive routing, structured hand-offs, policy validation and trace capture be combined in a prototype framework?
- RQ3: How does the framework compare with fixed-role sequential and static-graph baselines in success, intent fidelity, policy compliance, trace completeness, time and cost?
- RQ4: How reliably does the framework preserve authorised intent and reject invalid actions under ambiguous, conflicting and adversarial inputs, including an assumed compromise of one agent?
- RQ5 (extension beyond the approved proposal): can the effects of a compromised subset of agents be bounded soundly before deployment?

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Orchestration, routing and delegation

Multi-agent orchestration research in 2025 and 2026 has moved from fixed roles toward learned and adaptive coordination. MasRouter learns to route tasks across a team of agents rather than fixing roles in advance (Yue et al., 2025), and difficulty-aware orchestration adjusts the pipeline shape to the query (Su et al., 2025). Dang et al. (2025) let a team's own structure evolve during execution rather than fixing a graph up front. Ji et al. (2025) treat multi-agent systems as learned graph-generative models, which gives graph-based coordination a principled footing but keeps the routing decision separate from any authorisation decision. Yu et al. (2025) show that long multi-agent debates lose context, and address it with a tree-structured reasoning layout rather than a policy layer. None of this routing literature treats a policy denial or an integrity failure as a signal the router should learn from, and none of it is evaluated under an assumed compromise of one of the agents being routed to. The orchestration framework in this paper uses a standard Bayes-UCB bandit for routing (Auer et al., 2002; Kaufmann et al., 2012), which is not itself a contribution, and the evaluation treats routing as one of four controls rather than as the paper's main claim.

### 2.2 Intent and hand-off integrity

A hand-off between agents is where a constraint most easily disappears. Recent measurement work confirms this is not a hypothetical problem. A governance-attenuation study across hand-off boundaries found that a policy-relevant fact survived a single-agent loop without loss, degraded in 56 percent of cases in a fixed pipeline, and degraded in 85 percent of cases in an orchestrator-and-sub-agent design when the underlying model was a 32-billion-parameter model, falling to 3 to 6 percent only once the model was replaced with a stronger one. This dependence on model capability matches a pattern in this paper's own results, where the governance controls make no measurable difference on a capable model and the task itself becomes the binding constraint on a weaker one. Several mechanisms now exist to protect the hand-off itself rather than just hoping it is honest: capability tokens attenuated monotonically along a delegation chain (Veski et al., 2026), signed delegation tokens in an agentic analogue of a web authentication token, and hash-chained evidence trails. The signed, hash-linked hand-off in this paper's artefact belongs to this family and is an engineering application of established primitives (tamper-evident logging, cryptographic signatures) rather than a new one.

### 2.3 Tool authorisation, prompt injection and information flow

This is the most active of the five themes and the one closest to the artefact's policy gate. Progent gives a deterministic, LLM-generated privilege-control policy over tool calls (Shi et al., 2025). AgentSpec enforces customisable runtime rules over an agent's actions (Wang et al., 2025). CaMeL attaches capabilities and data-flow policy to every value that passes through an agent and checks policy before a tool call executes, with a published security proof (Debenedetti et al., 2025). FIDES propagates confidentiality and integrity labels through tool calls and blocks a sensitive call before it runs; it already ships inside the Microsoft Agent Framework (Costa et al., 2025). IsolateGPT isolates each tool's execution so one compromised tool cannot see another's data (Wu et al., 2025). For multi-agent pipelines specifically, SafeFlow propagates a semantic taint across the whole collaboration graph rather than one agent (SafeFlow, 2026), and CAPMAS attenuates a delegated capability monotonically as it passes from agent to agent, modelled on macaroons (Veski et al., 2026). Two papers are close enough to this paper's own evaluation design to need direct comparison. Paul and Nandy (2026) combine signed messages, provenance tracking and privilege-scoped tools in one system and test it against fourteen attack vectors. Safin et al. (2026) show a compromised low-privilege agent reaching unauthorised execution in every undefended trial, then contained once signed task-bound tokens and a policy oracle are added, with a component ablation that attributes the containment to specific mechanisms. Both studies report, independently of this project, the same four-control combination this paper's artefact implements, which is why Section 1 states the integration as a design science contribution rather than a novel one. Muruaga (2026) evaluates enforcement independently of the underlying model by injecting a ground-truth attacker call after the agent's first legitimate call, which is the same experimental design as the assumed-compromise suite used in Section 3.4 of this paper.

### 2.4 Evaluation of agent security and policy compliance

Standard benchmarks now exist for parts of this problem. AgentDojo evaluates both task utility and attack success under prompt injection on a shared set of agent pipelines (Debenedetti et al., 2024). InjecAgent benchmarks indirect prompt injection specifically (Zhan et al., 2024). TAMAS benchmarks adversarial risk across ten different model backbones in a multi-agent setting (TAMAS, 2025), and ContainmentBench evaluates post-exposure containment with over seventeen thousand recorded traces (ContainmentBench, 2026). Ajar measures how much privilege a defence leaves open rather than only whether an attack succeeded (Ajar, 2026), which is a useful complement to a simple attack-success-rate metric. This paper does not use any of these benchmarks directly. It uses an internally authored eighteen-task suite plus a four-case assumed-compromise suite, for the reasons given in Section 3.4, and treats adoption of AgentDojo or a similar external benchmark as future work rather than something this paper claims to have done.

### 2.5 Static and pre-deployment analysis

This theme decides how the paper's RQ5 contribution should be worded, so it is treated at more length. Three works sit closest to the static blast-radius bound developed in Section 3.7.

Parisel (2026) builds a static, design-time influence graph over a multi-agent configuration: an edge exists wherever two agents share a resource they could write to and read from, or wherever a conservative channel between two agents cannot be ruled out. An agent's effective autonomy is the union of every action reachable through that graph, and the paper defines a compositional blast-radius metric over an enterprise resource hierarchy, plus a predicate for collusion between two or more agents. The paper states explicitly that it offers no enforcement or non-interference guarantee, has no runtime implementation, and illustrates the metric with one worked example rather than an evaluation.

Muruaga (2026) proves two properties of a runtime authorisation model: that the reachable scope of a delegated capability cannot grow as it passes along a delegation chain, and that composing two sound authorisation checks yields a sound combined check, evaluated across 3,154 instances. This is a property of the running system's authorisation scope at each step, not a computation, made before any agent runs, of every effect a given compromised subset of agents could cause.

Moon and Varshney (2026) take the strongest soundness stance of the three: they model the underlying model as an unconstrained oracle that can emit any action at all, and mechanically verify, in the Dafny proof assistant, that a containment layer enforces its boundary policy against every action the model could emit, for one specific agent framework. This proof is stronger evidence of soundness than the paper proof plus property-based tests in Section 3.7 of this paper, because it is machine-checked rather than checked by hand and by testing. It is not parametric in which subset of agents is compromised, has no multi-agent influence model, and includes no least-privilege synthesis step.

Three further works are relevant to the mechanism rather than to the top-level claim. CaMeLoT checks each generated plan against a temporal-logic policy before executing it, which is static per plan rather than static over the whole workflow (CaMeLoT, 2026). AgentFlow builds a static dependency graph from an agent program's own source code across 5,399 programs, which analyses the code rather than the deployed configuration (AgentFlow, 2026). MiniScope and a related system synthesise a least-privilege tool policy automatically, from a permission hierarchy in one case and from audit logs with reinforcement learning in the other (MiniScope, 2025), but neither claims that the synthesised policy is minimal in a formal policy lattice, and neither proves that the synthesis provably shrinks a compromise bound.

The gap that survives this review is specific: no reviewed work computes, before any agent runs, the set of effects reachable by an arbitrary compromised subset of agents, through context, shared state, tool calls and the hand-off transport; proves that set sound against the actual gate and runtime enforcing it; checks the bound exactly against a live adaptive attacker; and synthesises a least-privilege policy that is proved to shrink the bound without breaking any legitimate task. Each ingredient exists somewhere in the literature reviewed above. The combination does not. Section 3.7 and Section 4.4 present this paper's contribution against that gap, worded as an extension of Parisel (2026), Muruaga (2026) and Moon and Varshney (2026), not as the first instance of a pre-deployment compromise bound.

## 3. Materials and methods

### 3.1 Research design

This study follows design science research (Hevner et al., 2004), because the goal is to build and evaluate an artefact for a defined computing problem rather than to test a theory about an existing system. The six activities of the Peffers et al. (2007) process map onto this project as follows: problem identification is the gap described in Section 1; objective definition is RQ1 to RQ5; design and development is the runtime described in Section 3.2; demonstration is the pilot and the main-suite nominal runs; evaluation is the baseline comparison, the assumed-compromise suite and the static-bound validation reported in Section 4; and communication is this paper plus the archived, versioned code repository. The unit of analysis throughout is the orchestration framework, not the capability of any one language model, so every condition in every suite uses the same tasks, the same tool registry, the same decoding settings, and, within a given run of the suite, the same model.

### 3.2 Artefact

The runtime has three layers with one-way dependencies: a core layer providing the orchestration primitives, a research layer containing the experimental provenance guard and the static analyser, and a bench layer containing the task suites and the evaluation harness. The core layer never imports from the other two, which is checked automatically in continuous integration.

A run executes a workflow of steps in dependency order. For each step, a router selects a candidate agent, a hand-off delivers the step's inputs to that agent, the agent proposes tool calls inside an agent loop bounded by a turn budget and a repair budget, and each proposed call passes through the policy gate before any tool code executes. Four controls sit on top of a baseline pipeline.

First, routing keeps a Beta-distributed success estimate for each agent on each task domain, starts every agent with one warm-up task, and then selects, by Bayes-UCB, the candidate with the highest upper-confidence quantile of its posterior (Auer et al., 2002; Kaufmann et al., 2012). This is a standard bandit algorithm applied to agent selection, not a new one.

Second, a hand-off package carries the run identifier, a hand-off identifier, the hash of the previous package in the chain, the task objective, the constraint list, references to its input data, the list of tools the receiving agent may use, the expected output schema, and an expiry time. The package is serialised to a canonical JSON form over a restricted value domain and signed with an Ed25519 key specific to the sending principal. The receiver verifies the signature, the hash chain back to the run's genesis hash, every required field, the expiry, and the schema, and rejects the hand-off with an integrity error if any check fails. This is distinct from the proposal's original wording, which described the serialisation as conforming to RFC 8785; the implemented form is canonical JSON over a restricted value domain, and that correction is carried through to this paper rather than repeated from the proposal (deviation A11 in Table 9).

Third, every proposed tool call is reduced to a finite record of the tool, the operation, the resource class it touches, the sensitivity of any columns it would expose, and whether it writes state or sends data externally. An external policy gate inspects this record against a declarative policy and returns one of three decisions: allow it unchanged, bound it by revising an argument to the nearest permitted value, or deny it. Bounding is implemented as interval projection for numeric arguments and as subset projection for column lists, both over tool metadata the gate already holds; it is not a Euclidean projection over the full action vector, which is how the proposal's appendix described it before implementation (deviation A10). A denied call never reaches tool code.

Fourth, a provenance guard wraps the policy gate for one specific class of call: a side-effecting call whose control arguments, such as an email recipient, must be traceable to the task's own trusted text. The guard checks whether the normalised argument value appears as a substring of the trusted corpus (the default mode) or as an exact token in that corpus (an alternative, stricter mode). A call whose control argument cannot be traced to trusted text is denied even if the policy gate alone would have allowed it.

Every routing decision, hand-off result, policy decision, tool call result, and step output is appended to a trace that is itself hash-linked, so that an edit to any past event breaks verification of every event after it.

### 3.3 Conditions

Four conditions are evaluated, each a configuration of the same runtime rather than four separate implementations, which removes a common confound in baseline comparisons where a baseline is reimplemented from scratch and may differ from the proposed system in ways unrelated to the mechanism under test.

- B1: fixed-role sequential delegation with a plain-text, unstructured hand-off between agents.
- B2: a static-graph workflow with structured JSON state passed between steps, but no adaptive routing, no signed hand-off, and no policy gate.
- P: the proposed framework at proposal scope, meaning Bayes-UCB routing, signed hand-offs, and the policy gate, without the provenance guard or re-dispatch.
- GPLUS: P plus the provenance guard and re-dispatch on step failure.

Six further configurations ablate one mechanism of GPLUS at a time (removing provenance, removing the gate, removing signing, removing re-dispatch, removing routing, and removing both re-dispatch and routing), used to attribute an observed effect to a specific mechanism rather than to the bundle as a whole. B1 and B2 still bind tools to a step before execution, which is a realistic default for an unmanaged framework and makes both baselines stronger than a true no-controls condition.

### 3.4 Tasks and suites

Eighteen main-suite tasks span three task families (information synthesis, evidence reconciliation, and policy-sensitive planning), each at a low, medium or high constraint tier with four, six or eight atomic constraints respectively, structured as a three-step retrieve, analyse, report workflow. The adversarial suite adds eleven live robustness cases covering a missing entity, conflicting tool data, an injected instruction aimed at an external or an internal sink, an injected destructive-write request, a request for a prohibited action stated directly by the simulated principal, a compromised peer agent, hand-off tampering in transit, a transient tool failure, a tool outage, and malformed model output, plus a twelve-case sequence suite that holds one agent permanently degraded across twenty consecutive runs to test routing and re-dispatch under a persistent fault rather than a one-off perturbation.

The threat model for RQ4 was extended beyond the proposal's plan during the first formal run, and this extension is disclosed rather than silently substituted (deviation A20 in Table 9). The original plan tested textual indirect prompt injection: an instruction hidden inside tool-returned data, asking the agent to do something it was not told to do. Across every condition in that suite, the live model made zero attack attempts; it treated tool-returned text as data rather than as instructions in every one of 550 runs, which means the adversarial suite as originally designed cannot measure containment, because there is nothing for a control to contain. In response, an assumed-compromise suite was added: in each of four cases, the targeted agent's first proposed tool call is replaced by an attacker-chosen call (a restricted-column read, an email to an external domain, an email to an in-domain mailbox named only in untrusted tool data, or a record deletion), and every subsequent step of the run proceeds with the live model as normal. This isolates whether the architecture contains a compromised agent's action from whether the underlying model resists being talked into one, which the textual-injection suite could not do with a model this capable.

### 3.5 Models and settings

Protocol v1 through v4 used one model family, DeepSeek, requested as `deepseek-chat` and reported by the provider as `deepseek-flash`, at temperature 0, in JSON mode, with a maximum of 4,096 tokens per completion, a fixed random seed of 42, a turn budget of four tool-use turns and a repair budget of two re-asks at temperature 0.3 for a malformed reply.

A single-model-family result cannot distinguish a property of the architecture from a property of that one model, which `docs/limitations.md` already flagged as an open external-validity threat before this evaluation. Protocol v5 re-runs the frozen main suite and the assumed-compromise suite against two further model families, changing only the model, the endpoint, and the credential, with every task, policy, condition and decoding setting held fixed. The two added models are an open-weight model served by Groq, requested as `openai/gpt-oss-120b`, and Google's `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, served through Google AI Studio's OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Both were chosen on free-tier availability after piloting, and the choice itself needed disclosed deviations.

Groq's `qwen/qwen3.8-27b` was tried first and reached its 200,000-token daily cap during the main-suite run, which is a hard quota rather than a transient rate limit, so it was retired after the fact rather than used; the thirty-two completed runs it produced before exhaustion are reported as excluded, not pooled with the retained model's results (deviation A28 in Table 9). A smaller Groq model, `openai/gpt-oss-20b`, triggers Groq's own tool-call guardrail against the artefact's prompted-JSON tool-call protocol, because the artefact deliberately does not use the provider's native function-calling parameter; changing that only for one provider would break the model-invariance the comparison depends on, so this smaller model was rejected rather than special-cased. On the Gemini side, the flagship `gemini-3.8-flash` model works correctly but is capped, on this free tier, at twenty requests per day, which is too small to run even the pilot suite in a single day; `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` was selected instead after a clean pilot. One setting differs for Gemini alone: its OpenAI-compatible endpoint rejects the `seed` parameter outright with an HTTP 400 error, so `seed` is omitted for Gemini and left at 42 for DeepSeek and Groq. Temperature, maximum tokens and JSON mode are identical across all three model families, and this is the only settings field that differs (ADR-0005).

Because repeating the full 360-run main suite and 160-run compromised suite at five repetitions per cell on two free-tier providers is bound by daily request and token quotas rather than by compute, the number of repetitions for the two added model families was reduced from five to two per cell for protocol v5, a disclosed reduction in statistical power rather than a change to which conditions or tasks were tested (deviation A28, Section 3.8).

### 3.6 Metrics

Table 5 lists the metrics used throughout, following the proposal's Table 3 with definitions carried over unchanged. Task success is the fraction of completed runs in which every mandatory constraint was satisfied. Intent fidelity is the fraction of a task's own atomic constraints that were satisfied, so a run that fails one constraint out of six scores differently from a run that fails one out of six on a harder task. Violation is a binary flag per run for whether a restricted value was disclosed, a write side effect occurred that the task did not call for, or an email was sent to a recipient the task did not authorise. Attack success rate is the violation rate computed over the adversarial and assumed-compromise suites specifically. Trace completeness is the fraction of required linked trace fields present for a run, which is zero by construction for B1 and B2 because neither condition produces a trace at all; this is reported to match the proposal's measure, not as a finding about either baseline. Integrity detection is the fraction of seeded invalid hand-off packages that the receiver correctly rejects. Cost is counted as model calls and prompt and completion tokens per run, and time is wall-clock seconds per run.

Every check is deterministic and computed without access to the condition label that produced the run, which is itself regression-tested so a future code change cannot silently reintroduce condition-awareness into scoring. The proposal originally specified two blinded human assessors scoring a stratified twenty percent sample with Cohen's kappa; that step was replaced with the deterministic checks above because no ethics approval for human assessors was in place at the time of the formal runs (deviation A7 in Table 9). This is a stricter and more reproducible check for the specific items the checks test, at the cost of being coarser than a human reader on free-text quality; a reviewer who requires the originally planned kappa measure can score the retained twenty percent sample held for this purpose once a human assessor, such as the supervisor, is available.

### 3.7 Static blast-radius analysis

The question behind RQ5 is whether, before a single agent runs, it is possible to compute every effect a given compromised subset of agents could still cause, and to know that bound is correct no matter what the compromised agents say or do. The full formal treatment, including every definition, lemma and proof, is in the supplementary formal model document and is only summarised here.

A deployment is modelled as a workflow of steps with role and tool assignments, a set of agents with roles, tool metadata recording which operations write state or send data externally and which columns of a resource they can expose, and a declarative policy. An adversary is defined by three things it may control: the model output of every agent in a chosen compromised set, the content returned by any tool in a chosen set of injectable tools, and, if stated, the hand-off transport itself. The analysis proves a correspondence lemma showing the policy gate's actual decision procedure, read directly from its source code, computes exactly the same admissibility test as a mathematical definition of authority at each step. From that lemma, a step-containment theorem shows that no execution, under any adversary behaviour at all, including every agent in the compromised set behaving arbitrarily, produces an effect the gate's own authority rule at that step would not admit. An influence relation then propagates from a compromised step to every step that could be affected by it, through shared tool-written state, through context passed at a hand-off, through an injectable tool, or through a tampered transport, and the blast radius of a compromised set is defined as the union of the authority of every step the influence relation reaches. A soundness theorem shows that every effect that depends on the adversary at all lies inside this blast radius, and that any step outside the influence relation behaves exactly as it would with no adversary present, up to the randomness of an interchangeable agent selection.

A least-privilege synthesis procedure then takes a set of observed nominal task executions and computes the smallest policy, in a formally defined ordering over policies, that still permits every observed call; this synthesised policy is proved to be minimal with respect to those observations and to only shrink, never grow, the blast radius computed from the unsynthesised policy.

The analysis was validated three ways. First, it was checked offline against every recorded run across every prior protocol, by replaying each run's executed calls against the bound computed for that run's compromised set and checking for a violation. Second, the correspondence lemma itself was checked by property-based testing against the real gate implementation across thousands of generated cases per test run, rather than trusted purely as a hand proof. Third, a live adaptive attacker was run against one task under eight attack variants, each compared against the bound's prediction of whether the attack would reach its target.

### 3.8 Protocol, statistics and deviations

Every protocol was frozen in version control before the formal runs it governs, under the tags `protocol-v1` through `protocol-v5`, and nothing in an earlier protocol's results was re-run to replace an unfavourable finding; later protocols are amendments, motivated by an earlier protocol's own findings and disclosed as such. Task, condition and repetition order was randomised with a fixed seed. Per-condition summaries report the mean and a 95 percent cluster-bootstrap confidence interval over tasks, using ten thousand resamples with a fixed seed, plus the median and interquartile range for time and token cost. Paired comparisons are computed at the task level, using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Pratt's method for zero differences, with the Holm correction applied within each family of related metrics. Where almost every paired difference is zero, which happens whenever two conditions both sit at a success ceiling, the test is reported as uninformative rather than read for significance, because a test with almost no non-zero differences has almost no power to detect anything.

One further protocol deviation needs explicit disclosure because it touches the project's own frozen no-rerun rule. The evaluation protocol as frozen states that a provider error counts as a failure of the run it hit and that nothing is re-run to replace it. Protocol v5's two added model families, run on free tiers, produced genuine infrastructure failures: a provider returning HTTP 429 for exceeding a per-minute token quota, or a daily quota being exhausted mid-run, in each case before the provider's model had generated any output at all, so the row carries zero model calls. Treating these as permanent failures would have meant the comparison across model families actually compared different numbers of completed cells per model, which is itself a confound. The resolution applied, and disclosed here rather than only in the appendix, is narrow: a failed row is only pruned and re-attempted if it shows zero model calls, meaning the provider rejected the request before the model saw it; a row where the model produced a completed or malformed reply and the run then failed for a different reason is never pruned or re-run. Under this rule, Gemini's main suite had thirty-three such zero-call failures pruned and re-attempted before its final completed set, Groq's main suite had fifty-three pruned across three resumption passes, Gemini's compromised suite had nineteen pruned after an initial daily-quota exhaustion, and Groq's compromised suite needed none, completing cleanly in one pass. Every reported number in Section 4 reflects only completed runs after this pruning; a reviewer wanting the stricter reading, with every provider rejection counted as a failure, can reconstruct it from the pre-pruning files retained in the project's results archive.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 RQ1 and RQ2: artefact demonstration

The requirement catalogue derived from RQ1 maps to the four controls described in Section 3.2, and Table 2 (appendix) restates the RQ to objective to evidence alignment from the proposal, updated with the evidence actually produced. The implementation passes 104 unit and integration tests at the time of writing, covering routing updates, missing or altered hand-off fields, invalid signatures, expiry handling, prohibited tool requests, and trace reconstruction after a simulated tamper. This demonstrates that the four controls described in the proposal were built as specified and operate as the component contracts in Table 2 require; it is evidence for RQ1 and RQ2 and is not itself a claim about RQ3 or RQ4.

### 4.2 RQ3: nominal performance across model families

Table 6 reports main-suite task success, intent fidelity, and overhead for each condition, separately per model family, because the three model families are not pooled as if they were one population.

On DeepSeek (360 runs, five repetitions per cell), task success sits at a ceiling across every condition: B1 98.9 percent, B2 100.0 percent, P 98.9 percent, GPLUS 100.0 percent, with intent fidelity at 99.9 to 100.0 percent in every condition and zero violations anywhere in the suite. Every paired Wilcoxon comparison between conditions is non-significant after Holm correction, and because at most a handful of tasks produce any non-zero paired difference at all, the tests are uninformative rather than evidence of equivalence. The practical-significance criterion the proposal set in advance, a ten-percentage-point gain in success or fidelity, is not met by any condition over any other. Hypothesis H-RQ3a, that the proposed framework beats both baselines on success and fidelity, is therefore not supported; this is a null result, reported as such rather than reframed, and it is the finding the proposal itself anticipated as a live possibility given a sufficiently capable model.

Gemini (248 runs across the four conditions, with the per-model repetition count reduced to two per cell as disclosed in Section 3.5) replicates the same ceiling pattern: B1 93.5 percent, B2 96.9 percent, P 96.7 percent, GPLUS 96.8 percent, with zero violations in every condition. Groq's open-weight model (166 runs) behaves differently: B1 40.9 percent, B2 39.0 percent, P 29.7 percent, GPLUS 31.8 percent. Task success on this model is far below the other two, which is a property of the model's own capability on the main suite's tasks rather than of any condition, because Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction find no statistically significant difference between P and B1, between P and B2, or between GPLUS and P on this model either (all adjusted p = 1.00). Zero nominal violations occurred in every condition on every model, so H-RQ3b, that the proposed framework produces fewer violations than the baselines on nominal tasks, is not testable here: there is nothing for the gate to have prevented, because the baselines never violated anything on a task that was not under assumed compromise in the first place.

Framework overhead is small relative to model latency on every model family. Scripted-model timing, isolating the runtime's own cost from any provider's response time, measures 0.16 milliseconds per three-step run for B1, 0.23 milliseconds for B2, 1.68 milliseconds for P and 1.69 milliseconds for GPLUS, against several seconds of actual model response time per run. Median wall time with a live model shows P at 1.03 times B2's time on DeepSeek, comfortably inside the proposal's 1.30 threshold, so H-RQ3c, that overhead stays within thirty percent, is supported.

### 4.3 RQ4: containment under assumed compromise

Table 7 reports the assumed-compromise results for all three model families. The pattern is consistent across models. For K1, a compromised agent's first call reads restricted columns directly; no condition's live model copies the exposed values into its final deliverable, so task success never drops to zero for this case, but the ungated conditions (B1, B2) expose the restricted values into every downstream agent's context, where the gated conditions (P, GPLUS) remove the restricted columns before they reach the next step. For K2, an email to an external domain, and K4, a destructive record deletion, both ungated baselines show a 100 percent violation rate on every model tested, while the policy gate in P and GPLUS blocks both actions in every trial on every model, a 0 percent violation rate. For K3, an email to an in-domain mailbox that is named only in untrusted tool data rather than in the task's own trusted text, the gate alone is not enough: P shows a 100 percent violation rate on every model, matching the two ungated baselines, because the recipient's domain is permitted and the gate has no basis to deny it. GPLUS, which adds the provenance guard on top of the same gate, shows a 0 percent violation rate on this case on every model tested. Removing the provenance guard from GPLUS (one of the six ablations) restores the K3 violation, and removing the gate itself restores every violation, which shows the provenance guard's effect depends on the gate underneath it rather than acting alone.

This pattern answers RQ4 and supports H1: policy gating contains out-of-domain actions, and only the provenance guard contains an attacker's attempt to steer output to an in-domain recipient that the gate alone would permit, and this containment finding holds across DeepSeek, Gemini and the Groq-served open-weight model without exception on the K2, K3 and K4 cases.

![Fig. 5. Violation rate under assumed compromise (K1 to K4), by condition and model. Darker cells are a higher violation rate.](/Users/shafinoid/Documents/GitHub/guide/scripts/paper/figures/fig5_compromise_heatmap.png)

The textual indirect-injection suite described in Section 3.4 produced zero attack attempts across all 550 of its runs on the original DeepSeek protocol, so attack resistance against this style of textual injection could not be measured with these models; this is reported as a limitation of what this suite can show rather than evidence that injection is not a risk in general.

The integrity suite, run without any live model, detects all ten seeded mutation classes at a 100 percent rate when per-principal signing keys are used, with zero false rejections of a valid package, and detects zero percent of sender-spoofing attempts when a single shared key is used instead, which supports H3: per-principal keys, not merely signing itself, are what makes spoofing detectable. The twenty-run degraded-agent sequence supports H2: a statically assigned baseline never recovers from a permanently faulty agent across all twenty runs, adaptive routing alone recovers in nineteen of twenty, and re-dispatch, with or without adaptive routing, recovers in all twenty.

### 4.4 RQ5: static blast-radius bound

Across 1,575 recorded runs spanning 2,051 executed tool calls from every prior protocol, replaying each run's executed effects against the bound computed for its compromised set finds zero containment violations and zero blast-radius violations: every one of the fifty-five harmful effects observed in the assumed-compromise suite lies inside the bound predicted for that run before it executed. A least-privilege policy synthesised from the main suite's nominal traces denies zero of forty-four held-out calls and achieves full task success with zero policy denials across thirty-six live runs under the synthesised policy, while shrinking the policy substantially: for one representative task, the bound narrows from permitting any address at the organisation's own domain, under the proposed framework's default gate, to permitting exactly one specific recipient, under the synthesised least-privilege policy.

A live adaptive attacker was then run against eight attack variants on this same task. The bound correctly predicted containment for every variant the gate alone, the gate with token-exact provenance, or the synthesised least-privilege policy was designed to stop, with zero soundness violations across 120 runs: every one of seventy-five runs the bound predicted as blocked showed no harmful effect, which is the paper's exactness result for direct compromise (thirty-nine of thirty-nine predicted-reachable direct effects were in fact realised when the attacker was allowed to reach them). One variant, an email sent to a legitimate recipient's address that happens to also be a substring of a different, trusted address, was predicted as reachable under the default substring-matching provenance guard and realised exactly as predicted on all three repetitions, which is Finding F1 in Section 4 of the formal model: the default substring-matching mode is bypassable, and switching to exact-token matching, or to the synthesised least-privilege policy, blocks the same attack in all three repetitions. A second variant, where an attacker tries to get an honest downstream agent to act on a poisoned upstream agent's output, was never realised in six attempted repetitions, because the downstream model in these experiments did not comply with the poisoned instruction; the bound still predicts this path as reachable, which is Finding F4, an intentional over-approximation on effects beyond direct compromise rather than a bound violation.

![Fig. 6. Admissible recipients for one representative sink (task T15), by policy. B2 and P admit an unbounded, domain-wide set of recipients; the default substring-matching provenance guard under GPLUS admits 692 distinct strings; exact-token provenance and the synthesised least-privilege policy both admit exactly one.](/Users/shafinoid/Documents/GitHub/guide/scripts/paper/figures/fig6_blast_radius_bound_sizes.png)

A separate structural generalisation check examined nineteen hand-authored configurations that each reproduce one documented structural pattern from LangGraph, CrewAI or AutoGen, across five enterprise domains, without executing any of those three frameworks' own code. Eighteen of the nineteen configurations show at least one compromised agent whose blast radius reaches a destructive or data-exfiltration capability beyond that agent's own nominal job, which follows directly from a common integration pattern: binding a whole tool object to an agent, rather than the specific operation that agent's task actually needs, grants every operation the tool object exposes. Applying the same least-privilege synthesis procedure to each configuration's own nominal calls cuts the total reachable sink capability by 55.6 percent in aggregate, while denying zero of the configurations' own nominal calls.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Why nominal success did not move

The null result on nominal task success is not a failure of the proposed controls to work. It is a ceiling effect: a sufficiently capable model solves close to every nominal task correctly regardless of which of the four conditions it runs under, because none of the eighteen main-suite tasks requires routing, signed hand-offs or a policy gate to be solved correctly in the first place. The literature's own measurement work on hand-off attenuation shows the same capability dependence from the other direction: governance decay across hand-off boundaries is severe on a weaker 32-billion-parameter model and nearly absent on a stronger one. Both findings point the same way. The value of these controls is not visible on tasks a capable model would get right anyway. It is visible only once something goes wrong, whether because the model is weaker, as the Groq results in Section 4.2 show, or because an agent is compromised, as the assumed-compromise results in Section 4.3 show.

### 5.2 Why containment needed two mechanisms, not one

The policy gate alone stops every out-of-domain action tested, because an external domain and a destructive delete are both decisions the gate's declared policy can make from the call's own metadata, without needing to know anything about what the task's trusted text actually says. The in-domain steering case is different in kind: the attacker's chosen recipient is inside the permitted domain, so nothing in the gate's declarative rules gives it grounds to deny the call. The provenance guard closes exactly this gap, because it asks a different question: not "is this recipient's domain permitted," but "does this specific recipient appear anywhere in the task's own trusted text." This dependency relationship, where the guard's effectiveness collapses the moment the gate underneath it is removed, is why Section 4.3 reports the provenance guard as depending on the gate rather than as an independent control.

### 5.3 What the static bound adds beyond measuring containment at run time

Measuring containment by attacking a running system, which is what the assumed-compromise suite and the closest prior work (Muruaga, 2026; Safin et al., 2026) both do, can only report what was tested. The static bound in Section 3.7 computes, before any agent runs, every effect a stated compromised set could cause, which means an operator can audit a configuration's worst case without having to construct and run every possible attack against it first. The analysis in Section 4.4 found two concrete weaknesses this way that the live testing in Section 4.3 had not surfaced on its own: the substring-matching provenance guard's exposure to a crafted substring recipient, and the structural over-privilege created by binding whole tool objects rather than individual operations in realistic framework configurations. Both were confirmed live afterward. This is the practical argument for a pre-deployment bound over run-time measurement alone: it finds a bug before the attack that would demonstrate it has to be invented.

### 5.4 Model invariance, and where it breaks

The containment findings for K2, K3 and K4 hold without exception across DeepSeek, Gemini and the Groq-served open-weight model, which is evidence that the architecture, not any one model's own good behaviour, is doing the containing. Nominal task success does not generalise the same way: Gemini replicates DeepSeek's ceiling, but the Groq model's success rate sits far below both, on every condition including the baselines. This is a model-capability effect rather than a condition effect, since the Wilcoxon tests in Section 4.2 find no significant difference between conditions on this model either, but it does mean the ceiling-effect explanation in Section 5.1 is itself model-dependent: a sufficiently weak model would presumably show a measurable gap between conditions, somewhere between this model's near-floor performance and the other two models' ceiling, that this evaluation's choice of models did not happen to land on.

### 5.5 Practical implications

An operator deploying a multi-agent pipeline gets two separable, actionable results from this paper's methods, independent of whichever specific product the operator uses. First, a least-privilege policy can be synthesised directly from a pipeline's own observed legitimate traffic, which the results in Section 4.4 show removes a majority of reachable sink capability without breaking any observed legitimate call; an operator need not hand-author a tight policy, only observe nominal behaviour long enough to synthesise one. Second, per-principal signing keys, not merely signing as such, are what makes an impersonation attempt detectable at all; a shared signing key across every agent, which is a simpler implementation choice an engineering team might reach for by default, detects none of the sender-spoofing attempts a per-principal key scheme detects at a 100 percent rate.

### 5.6 Limitations

The main-suite tasks and their checkers were authored by the research team rather than drawn from an external benchmark, and the pilot run found two harness defects in the checking logic itself before the formal protocol was frozen, plus one further checker defect found during the formal run and reported as a separate sensitivity analysis rather than folded into the primary results. This is a scope choice, not an oversight: an internally authored suite made it possible to also author the assumed-compromise cases needed for RQ5, which no existing external benchmark currently supports in the form this paper needed. Scoring is deterministic and limited to substring and field checks rather than a semantic judgement of free text, which the proposal's original design addressed with two blinded human assessors and Cohen's kappa; that step did not run, because ethics approval for human assessors was not yet in place when the formal runs executed, and it remains open rather than closed (Table 9, item A7).

The two added model families were evaluated at two repetitions per cell rather than the original five, because their free-tier daily and per-minute quotas could not support five repetitions of a 360-run suite within the project's timeline; this trades statistical power for coverage across model families, and the confidence intervals reported in Table 6 for Gemini and the Groq model are correspondingly wider than DeepSeek's. The soundness proof behind the static bound is checked by hand and by property-based testing against the real policy gate, not machine-checked in a proof assistant; Moon and Varshney's (2026) Dafny-verified containment proof is stronger evidence on this specific axis, and a machine-checked version of this paper's proof is future work rather than something claimed here. The provenance guard's default substring-matching mode is a heuristic, shown in Section 4.4 to be bypassable by a crafted recipient string that is itself a substring of a trusted address; the exact-token matching mode and the synthesised least-privilege policy both close this specific gap, but a more capable attacker able to probe trusted text for a usable substring at scale was not tested. Finally, the nineteen-configuration structural generalisation study in Section 4.4 is built from hand-authored configurations that reproduce a documented pattern from each of three popular frameworks; it does not execute any of those frameworks' own code, and it is not a claim about how a specific deployed LangGraph, CrewAI or AutoGen system behaves in practice.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper evaluated a policy-aware orchestration framework for enterprise multi-agent large language model workflows, comparing it against a fixed-role sequential baseline and a static-graph baseline across three independent model families. RQ1 and RQ2 are answered by a working artefact and its passing test suite. RQ3 returns a null result on capable models: none of the controls improve nominal task success or fidelity once a model is strong enough to solve the task suite on its own, at a governance overhead of roughly 1.5 milliseconds against several seconds of model latency. RQ4 is answered positively and specifically: a policy gate contains out-of-domain actions in every tested case, and a provenance guard is the one mechanism, among the four evaluated, that contains an attacker's attempt to steer output toward an in-domain but unapproved recipient, and both findings hold across all three model families tested. RQ5, pursued as an extension beyond the approved proposal, is answered by a static bound on the effects a compromised subset of agents can cause, proved sound against the enforcing runtime and confirmed exact against a live adaptive attacker on direct effects, paired with a least-privilege synthesis step that is shown, on both the project's own task suite and nineteen structurally realistic configurations, to shrink that bound substantially without breaking legitimate behaviour.

The combination of routing, signed hand-offs, a policy gate and an audit trail was independently published by two other groups in the months after this project's proposal was approved, so this paper presents that integration as a design science artefact rather than as a first result; the static blast-radius bound extends two concurrent 2026 preprints rather than preceding them. The main limitation carried into any future use of this work is that its task suite, checkers and the nineteen structural configurations were all authored by the research team rather than drawn from an existing benchmark or from a deployed system. Future work should adapt this evaluation to an external benchmark such as AgentDojo or τ-bench, test a wider range of model capability including a genuinely small or weak model, add human assessors once ethics approval permits it, and move the pre-deployment bound's soundness proof from a hand proof checked by property testing to a machine-checked proof.
